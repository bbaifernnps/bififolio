01

Overview

TrueMoney is a wallet millions of Thai people already trust for everyday money — but moving money across a border is a different game, with different rules. I own the full international transfer domain, outbound (sending abroad) and inbound (receiving from abroad), and I'm designing it end to end while the regulatory ground keeps shifting underneath it. It's the single biggest-scope project I own, and the one I'd point to as my name card.

02

The problem

Sending or receiving money internationally is inherently higher-stakes than a domestic transfer — more steps, more compliance checks, more ways for a user to feel unsure whether their money is safe. TrueMoney's existing patterns were built for fast, simple, domestic flows (P2P, PromptPay, merchant QR). International transfer needed to inherit that same feeling of an effortless wallet, while actually carrying far more regulatory weight than anything else in the domain.

Regulatory + UX tensionBank of Thailand compliance requirements vs. a simple, trustworthy wallet flow

03

Goals & constraints

Design a cross-border transfer experience that meets Bank of Thailand regulation without feeling like a compliance form. The real constraint here isn't technical — it's that BOT requirements don't stay still. Over a long build, rules around verification, limits and reporting shifted more than once, so the design had to stay flexible enough to absorb regulatory change without a full rebuild every time.

04

My process

1

Discover

Audited how cross-border transfer is handled elsewhere — global remittance-first apps (Revolut, Wise-style flows, Monzo, N26) alongside Thai banks (KBANK, SCB, Krungthai) and Alipay, to see how each balances trust, speed and compliance friction.

2

Define

Mapped outbound and inbound as two related but distinct journeys, and pinpointed exactly where BOT compliance touches the flow — verification, purpose-of-transfer, transaction limits — so those moments could be designed for, not bolted on.

3

Design

Built the flows inside TrueMoney's existing design system so international transfer feels like the same app, not a separate product — working with the DS team on any new components the compliance steps required.

4

Test & refine

Kept iterating as requirements moved: re-checking flows each time a rule changed, and designing the compliance touchpoints modularly so a future rule change costs a component update, not a redesign.

Process artefactsCompetitor audit, journey maps — coming with the Figma pass

05

The solution

One wallet, two directions. The send-abroad flow reuses the calm, one-decision-per-screen pattern from TrueMoney's domestic transfers (see the prototype below), while the receive-from-abroad flow gives people clear, plain-language visibility into a payment that started outside the app entirely — including the compliance checkpoints, framed as reassurance rather than red tape.

08

What I learned

Designing against a moving regulatory target taught me to build flexibility into the system itself, not just the screens — modular compliance components that can absorb a rule change without a redesign. It also sharpened how I frame trade-offs to non-design stakeholders: BOT requirements aren't negotiable, so the job is finding where craft still has room to make a mandatory step feel human.
